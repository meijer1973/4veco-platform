# Adaptation notes — reasoning transfer across §1.1.1, §1.1.2 and §1.1.3

## Shared product grammar

All three successful games preserve:

- compact source pane;
- clear but non-answer-giving goal;
- three short tasks;
- one-click interactions;
- local undo/replace;
- meaningful distractors;
- stable layout;
- feedback after checking;
- final exam-style answer assembly.

## Re-derived reasoning grammars

### §1.1.1 — scarcity and opportunity cost

```text
limited means → choice → best alternative given up
```

Natural interaction: evidence selection, best-alternative choice, concise answer construction.

### §1.1.2 — percentages and index figures

```text
numerical difference → unit → reference value → corrected claim
```

Natural interaction: unit classification, equivalent-route selection, claim repair.

### §1.1.3 — graphs and tables

```text
relevant graph evidence → observation versus estimate → scope of conclusion
```

Natural interaction: graph-point evidence selection, claim-strength judgement, evidence-based answer repair.

## What must not transfer into §1.1.3

- a scarcity-choice mechanic;
- a formula-selection worksheet;
- a causal flowchart;
- graph construction as the main game;
- a generic multiple-choice quiz;
- one textarea containing the whole response.

## Why this remains a reasoning game

The arithmetic is deliberately simple. The central questions are:

- Which observations are relevant?
- Is the statement observed, estimated or unsupported?
- Does the conclusion stay within the evidence?

## Transfer success criterion

The transfer succeeds when the games feel like one product family while demanding different mental work. It fails when agents copy the visible mechanic rather than deriving the paragraph's reasoning grammar.
